How the Almanac works

Where the numbers come from, how each one is checked, and what the words and colors on every city page mean.

The records

Each city page uses one official long-term record: the daily high, low, precipitation and snowfall that NOAA keeps for the city, read through RCC-ACIS (NOAA's Regional Climate Centers' data service). Most are "ThreadEx" city records, which join the city's observing sites over the years into one series, usually ending at the main airport. Temperatures are whole degrees Fahrenheit, exactly as recorded.

The Almanac covers {{N_PLACES}} official records. Each page is rebuilt every day; the last 21 days are re-read each time, because stations post and correct values for a while after the day ends. A day's values are final the morning after.

Ranks and "typical"

A day's rank counts the years that were strictly warmer, plus one, so tied years share a rank: if two years were warmer and one tied, the day is "3rd warmest (tied)". Every rank says how many years it's out of, because 3rd of 40 and 3rd of 150 are very different.

Only days in the warmest or coolest fifth of their date's history get a rank. Everything in between reads "typical for" the date, because a middle rank ("71st warmest") says nothing useful.

Complete years

A month counts when no more than 9 of its days are missing. A year counts when all 12 months do. A year's average is the average of its 12 monthly means, so a gap in one season can't tilt the year warmer or cooler. Yearly and decade figures use complete years only.

Trends, and signal or noise

A trend is a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years. Each trend comes with a 95% range: how far the true trend could plausibly be from the line, given how much years bounce around.

  • Signal (orange): the whole 95% range is above zero, or below it. The change is real, not luck.
  • At the edge (light orange): the range is at least 80% of the way to clearing zero.
  • Noise (gray): the range doesn't come close to clearing zero, so there's no clear change.

Orange always means a real signal on these pages; nothing else is orange.

Every change is checked three ways

Before a page says a place has warmed or cooled, the build checks the claim three ways: the trend across the full record, the last 30 complete years against the first 30, and the trend over the last 40 years. When the full record and the last 40 years disagree, the headline names the period instead of picking one ("Chicago's nights have warmed since 1986. Its days warmed over the long run.").

A page whose claims fail a check isn't published that day; the last page that passed stays up. A place needs at least 60 complete years for the first-versus-last check, so a few short or gappy records have no page yet.

Records against chance

If a place's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding each date's record. The pages count the daily records set since 2000 that still stand and compare that with what equal chances would give.

To tell whether a gap is real, the build picks the same number of years at random from the place's own record, 20,000 times, keeping whole years together so a cold snap that set several records counts as one event. If fewer than 2.5% of those picks do as well as the real years since 2000, the gap is a signal; under 6% is at the edge. "Far fewer" needs half or less of the expected count and a signal.

The map's colors

  • Orange: half or fewer of the record lows chance would give, and the gap is real.
  • Light orange: fewer than chance, but not that far.
  • Gray: about what chance would give.
  • Teal: more record lows than chance. Only a handful of places; check a city's station history before reading much into it.

Normals

"Normal" is the 1991–2020 average for the date, from the same record, smoothed over 15 days so one odd year doesn't make a bump in the calendar. The normal range on the year-so-far chart runs from the average low to the average high.

Rain, snow and dry spells

Precipitation is rain plus melted snow, in inches; yearly totals use complete years only. A dry spell is a run of days with less than 0.01 inch: a trace counts as dry, and a missing day ends the spell. Each page shows spells that are long for that place, the shortest of 14, 30, 60 or 90 days that leaves no more than about one and a half spells a year.

Snowfall is shown without a trend: over a century, snow has been measured less consistently than rain (different boards, times and rules), so a long-run comparison wouldn't be reliable. Places that average under an inch a winter, or have fewer than 30 complete winters, don't show the snowfall chart.

The 7-day forecast

The top of each city page shows the National Weather Service forecast for the official record's own location: each day's forecast high, the low that night, the chance of precipitation and the Weather Service's words. It's fetched every time the pages are rebuilt, several times a day, and the page says when it was issued; a forecast more than 36 hours old is never shown.

When a forecast would stand out in the place's own record, a note says where it would land ("Would be the 2nd warmest Nov 21 high in 134 years"), using the same ranks as everywhere else. A night's low is ranked against the next date, because the official low of a day is usually set near dawn. Days 6 and 7 are marked less certain: forecasts lose accuracy quickly after a few days.

Unadjusted readings and station moves

These are the official records as kept, without adjustments. A record that joins several sites can shift when the station moves (most often from a downtown rooftop to a cooler, more open airport), when instruments change, or when the city grows around the station (which mostly warms nights). NOAA's adjusted climate datasets correct for these; the Almanac doesn't, so treat any single number as approximate.

A common fingerprint of a city-to-airport move is a record where nights cooled over the long run but have warmed since 1986. Pages that show it name the period in their headline, and the last 40 years are the clearer guide there.

Which places are included

Every official long-term city record that NOAA's data service reports and that is still updated: {{N_PLACES}} records, of which {{N_PAGES}} have pages today. A few places also have a volunteer-station record (Goshen, Indiana, for example) with a switch to the nearest official city. Places whose record is too short or too gappy for the checks above wait for more data rather than publish a claim that can't be checked.
