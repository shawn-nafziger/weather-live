3 · The record book

Record highs keep coming. Record lows have nearly stopped.

If Miami's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Miami has set 6 standing record lows, far fewer than the 76 that chance alone would give, and 188 record highs, far more than the 76 that chance alone would give. The 2020s hold the most record highs of any decade (87). Miami's all-time high is 100°F, on July 21, 1942.

4 · How it's changed

Miami has warmed, days and nights alike

Across 121 complete years: Miami's average low has risen +0.30°F per decade (95% range +0.22 to +0.37), about +3.8°F since 1896. Miami's average high has risen +0.31°F per decade (95% range +0.26 to +0.37), about +4.0°F since 1896.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Miami has gotten wetter

Miami's yearly precipitation has risen +0.85 inches per decade (95% range +0.24 to +1.46), about +10.8 inches since 1898. The 1991–2020 normal is 67.4 inches a year. The wettest year was 1959 (89.33 in); the driest, 1907 (33.15 in). Miami almost never sees snow (the 1991–2020 average is 0.0 inches a winter), so there's no snowfall chart.
