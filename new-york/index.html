3 · The record book

New York has nearly stopped setting record lows

If New York's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, New York has set 12 standing record lows, far fewer than the 62 that chance alone would give, and 76 record highs, close to the 62 that chance alone would give. The 1990s hold the most record highs of any decade (39). New York's all-time high is 106°F, on July 9, 1936.

4 · How it's changed

New York has warmed, days and nights alike

Across 157 complete years: New York's average low has risen +0.27°F per decade (95% range +0.22 to +0.31), about +4.1°F since 1869. New York's average high has risen +0.32°F per decade (95% range +0.27 to +0.36), about +4.9°F since 1869.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

New York has gotten wetter

New York's yearly precipitation has risen +0.55 inches per decade (95% range +0.27 to +0.83), about +8.6 inches since 1869. The 1991–2020 normal is 49.5 inches a year. The wettest year was 1983 (80.56 in); the driest, 1965 (26.09 in).
