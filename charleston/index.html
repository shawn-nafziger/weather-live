3 · The record book

Charleston has nearly stopped setting record lows

If Charleston's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Charleston has set 37 standing record lows, far fewer than the 109 that chance alone would give, and 121 record highs, close to the 109 that chance alone would give. The 2010s hold the most record highs of any decade (56). Charleston's all-time high is 105°F, on August 1, 1999.

4 · How it's changed

Charleston's days and nights both warmed. Nights warmed faster.

Across 88 complete years: Charleston's average low has risen +0.48°F per decade (95% range +0.38 to +0.58), about +4.2°F since 1938. Charleston's average high has risen +0.24°F per decade (95% range +0.15 to +0.33), about +2.1°F since 1938.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Rain: no clear long-term change

Charleston's yearly precipitation shows no clear long-term change (+0.22 inches per decade, a range that includes zero). The 1991–2020 normal is 52.5 inches a year. The wettest year was 2015 (74.89 in); the driest, 1954 (30.31 in). Charleston almost never sees snow (the 1991–2020 average is 0.4 inches a winter), so there's no snowfall chart.
