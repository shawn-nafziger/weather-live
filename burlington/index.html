3 · The record book

Record highs and lows, by the decade that set them

If Burlington's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Burlington has set 46 standing record lows, fewer than the 76 that chance alone would give, and 72 record highs, close to the 76 that chance alone would give. The 1930s hold the most record highs of any decade (82). Burlington's all-time high is 111°F, on July 14, 1936.

4 · How it's changed

Burlington's nights got warmer. Its days didn't.

Across 121 complete years: Burlington's average low has risen +0.11°F per decade (95% range +0.02 to +0.20), about +1.4°F since 1901. Burlington's average high shows no clear change: −0.03°F per decade, a range (−0.12 to +0.07) that includes zero. July and August highs have edged down across the full record; the 1930s summers were the hottest in it.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Rain and snow: no clear long-term change

Burlington's yearly precipitation shows no clear long-term change (+0.13 inches per decade, a range that includes zero). The 1991–2020 normal is 37.6 inches a year. The wettest year was 2010 (54.13 in); the driest, 1956 (22.87 in).
