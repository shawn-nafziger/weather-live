3 · The record book

Record highs keep coming. Record lows have nearly stopped.

If Louisville's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Louisville has set 15 standing record lows, far fewer than the 64 that chance alone would give, and 114 record highs, far more than the 64 that chance alone would give. The 2010s hold the most record highs of any decade (55). Louisville's all-time high is 107°F, on July 24, 1901.

4 · How it's changed

Louisville's nights have warmed since 1986. Its days have warmed.

Across 153 complete years: Louisville's average low shows no clear change: +0.03°F per decade, a range (−0.04 to +0.09) that includes zero. Louisville's average high has risen +0.14°F per decade (95% range +0.09 to +0.20), about +2.2°F since 1873. The last 40 years tell a different story: since 1986, lows have risen +1.14°F per decade (95% range +0.74 to +1.54) and highs have risen +0.77°F per decade (95% range +0.32 to +1.21). The full record joins different observing sites over the years, and a move (for example, from the city to a cooler airport) can hide or exaggerate a long-run trend, so the recent decades are the clearer guide here.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Louisville has gotten wetter

Louisville's yearly precipitation has risen +0.45 inches per decade (95% range +0.16 to +0.73), about +6.8 inches since 1873. The 1991–2020 normal is 50.3 inches a year. The wettest year was 2018 (68.83 in); the driest, 1930 (23.88 in).
