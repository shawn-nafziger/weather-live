3 · The record book

Pittsburgh has nearly stopped setting record lows

If Pittsburgh's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Pittsburgh has set 32 standing record lows, far fewer than the 64 that chance alone would give, and 47 record highs, fewer than the 65 that chance alone would give. The 1880s hold the most record highs of any decade (56). Pittsburgh's all-time high is 103°F, on July 10, 1881.

4 · How it's changed

Pittsburgh's nights cooled over the long run but have warmed since 1986. Its days show no clear change.

Across 151 complete years: Pittsburgh's average low has fallen −0.14°F per decade (95% range −0.20 to −0.07), about −2.0°F since 1875. Pittsburgh's average high shows no clear change: −0.05°F per decade, a range (−0.11 to +0.01) that includes zero. It's close, but the range still includes zero. The last 40 years tell a different story: since 1986, lows have risen +0.59°F per decade (95% range +0.25 to +0.94) and highs show no clear change. The full record joins different observing sites over the years, and a move (for example, from the city to a cooler airport) can hide or exaggerate a long-run trend, so the recent decades are the clearer guide here.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Pittsburgh has gotten wetter

Pittsburgh's yearly precipitation has risen +0.28 inches per decade (95% range +0.09 to +0.48), about +4.4 inches since 1871. The 1991–2020 normal is 39.6 inches a year. The wettest year was 2018 (57.83 in); the driest, 1930 (22.65 in).
