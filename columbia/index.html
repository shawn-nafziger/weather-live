3 · The record book

Columbia has nearly stopped setting record lows

If Columbia's climate weren't changing, every year would have the same chance of holding a daily record. Since 2000, Columbia has set 23 standing record lows, far fewer than the 72 that chance alone would give, and 67 record highs, close to the 72 that chance alone would give. The 1930s hold the most record highs of any decade (59). Columbia's all-time high is 113°F, on July 12, 1954.

4 · How it's changed

Columbia's nights have warmed. Its days have warmed since 1986.

Across 135 complete years: Columbia's average low has risen +0.12°F per decade (95% range +0.05 to +0.19), about +1.6°F since 1890. Columbia's average high shows no clear change: +0.04°F per decade, a range (−0.04 to +0.11) that includes zero. The last 40 years tell a different story: since 1986, lows have risen +0.83°F per decade (95% range +0.44 to +1.23) and highs have risen +0.65°F per decade (95% range +0.17 to +1.13). The full record joins different observing sites over the years, and a move (for example, from the city to a cooler airport) can hide or exaggerate a long-run trend, so the recent decades are the clearer guide here.

How to read these
  • Trend: a straight line through every complete year. "Per decade" is how much it rises or falls every 10 years.
  • 95% range: how far the trend could plausibly be off, given how much years bounce around. If the whole range is above zero, the warming is real, not luck.
  • Signal, at the edge, noise: signal means the range clears zero; at the edge means it's at least 80% of the way there; noise means it doesn't come close.

5 · Rain and snow

Rain and snow: no clear long-term change

Columbia's yearly precipitation shows no clear long-term change (+0.27 inches per decade, a range that includes zero). The 1991–2020 normal is 41.4 inches a year. The wettest year was 1993 (62.49 in); the driest, 1901 (21.35 in).
